  1. Tap Play it out. The Lichess board opens with the position loaded, oriented your way, you to move.
  2. Board menu (☰ under the board) → Continue from here → Play with the computer.
  3. Set the level listed and convert it.

F

Play forward — the check before you commit

Run this first, ahead of the P scans. The P cards tell you something is wrong and ask you to find it. That is the half you are already good at — when a card forces a look, the look works. What goes wrong in games is that the look never fires: you choose a quiet move, never scan the position it creates, and a capture that was sitting there the whole time finally lands. This drill reproduces that, not the symptom.

Each card replays a run of moves you actually played, one at a time, opponent replies included. Before each is shown, pick your own move in your head. Then the move you played appears on the board. Picture the position it creates. Does anything hang to a capture? Answer, see the verdict, go on. Somewhere in most cards is a move that cost you a piece; you are not told which, and some threats appear early and only cash in later. About one card in five is a decoy where nothing ever hangs, so “safe” has to be a real answer.

299 cards (239 from hanging-material games, 60 decoys), 1747 decisions, of which 345 hang. Aim to decide in under ten seconds — the moves that cost you games took eight. Your hit rate here is practice feedback, not the outcome; the outcome is the hanging-material rate in your next games, and that is pre-registered in the README.

P

Priority — hanging material

The single biggest source of thrown-away wins, and the reason this set sits ahead of the endgame drills. Across 1,515 blitz games there are 239 middlegame moves you played while already winning by +1.5 or more, where a capture worth at least a pawn and a half was on the board and you played something else. They split into two different skills, so they're now two different drills.

Group A — the scan. 184 positions where the threat was already there before you moved. The question is answerable from the position alone: which of your squares is loose? Give yourself fifteen seconds, name the square out loud, then open the answer. 79% have exactly one; the rest have two. This is the one to run now.

Group B — the check before you commit. 55 positions where nothing was hanging until your move created it. The board shows the position before you moved and the move is named underneath — picture it, then find the punishment. Unique answer every time. Do these after A.

Neither group is played out against the computer. Fifteen seconds, name it, move on — the skill is the scan, not the calculation.
